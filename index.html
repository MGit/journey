Presence and Simple Joys

If there is a remedy, then what good is being unhappy? Just do it. And if there is none, then why bother?

adapted from SHANTIDEVA

Bodhicaryāvatāra 6.10, tr. L. D. Barnett, The Path of Light (1909)

Shantideva, an Indian Buddhist monk of the eighth century, packs a whole philosophy into two questions. If something can be done about your trouble, why be miserable? Do it. If nothing can be done, why be miserable? It changes nothing.

The verse does not tell you to stop caring, or to pretend all is well. It only points out that the misery is the part that does no work.

Acceptance, in this sense, is not giving up. It is putting down the one thing you were carrying that was never going to help, so that both hands are free for whatever can be done.

Optimism

Optimism is learned the way handwriting is: slowly, and by practicing it.

Are you an optimist? Most of us answer as if it were eye color: you are or you aren't.

While there are genetic predispositions, the evidence suggests something more hopeful, and more modest. When researchers pooled the studies that tried to make people more optimistic, the exercises worked, moderately. One of the best exercises was also the simplest: write for a few minutes daily about your life going as well as it realistically could—as if it already had.

Not a transformation, but a nudge you can give yourself, on any ordinary morning.

So the question is not whether I am an optimist or not, but rather if I have practiced it lately.

Stuck: Between Hunger and Thirst

“Do you ask why such flight does not help you? It is because you flee along with yourself.”

SENECA

Moral Letters 28.2, trans. R. M. Gummere

A change of scene was supposed to lift Lucilius's gloom, and it had not. His friend, the Roman philosopher Seneca, was not surprised. You can cross the sea, he told him, and your troubles will follow, because you take yourself along.

Before you change the place, try staying in it for a while. If you stay and the itch fades, it was never about the place. If it sharpens, the place may truly be wrong.

A restlessness that follows you everywhere is about you, and that is good news, because you can work on it wherever you are. One that lifts the moment you leave the building is telling you something real about the building.

About the author

Martin Guay, PhD

Martin Guay, PhD, is a student of wisdom, and a teacher to whoever is curious and up for a good conversation. He follows his mind wherever it leads, always looking for the essence of what works, and why. For him, curiosity is where wisdom starts: the willingness to ask one more question, and to keep asking until the answer is simple enough to live by.
